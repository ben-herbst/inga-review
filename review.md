## Setting the Scene.

{abbr}`LLMs (Large Language Models)` have become immensely powerful. So much so that people *say* 
'{abbr}`AI (Artificial Intelligence)`' but *think* {abbr}`LLMs (Large Language Models`. This is natural given how 
central communication is to the human condition. Humans depend on verbal, on written, and also the more abstract forms 
of communication such as poetry, music, painting, sculpture.  {abbr}`LLMs (Large Language Models` might only be a part 
of {abbr}`AI (Artificial Intelligence)` but it is a big part.

In just a few short years {abbr}`LLMs (Large Language Models` have upended society. I want to point out just a few more
recent developments, in no particular order. I'll return to them later on in the review. Let's start with the
perhaps, the most obvious.

### Jobs

The debate is not so much about whether {abbr}`AI (Artificial Intelligence)` *is* disrupting the job market, as about 
which sectors are being hit the hardest and what the future holds for humans. Some industry leaders feel that humans 
may become redundant, others sound a more cautionary note. But there is no doubt that the landscape is changing[^a1],
and even the experts don't always agree on where things are heading. There is no doubt that some sectors will be hit
harder than others with junior software developers particularly vulnerable. How does one become a senior software
developer without first being a junior developer[^a2]?

[^a1]: See, for example the following quote,
	> It starts at the bottom, and it keeps going up, ... And I don’t know where it stops.
	>
	>--- {abbr}`Umesh Ramakrishnan chief strategy officer at Kingley Gate (quoted by Lisa Eadicicco in CNN Business, 10 May, 2026)` 

[^a2]: 
	> According to one Stanford study, in the occupations most susceptible to greater automation, such as software engineering or customer service, 
	> entry-level employment has already declined by 16 percent relative to the least-exposed roles.
	>
	>--- {abbr}`David M. Soloman, Chairman and Chief Executive of Goldman Sachs (New York Times, 22 May, 2026)`

The idea is that {abbr}`AI (Artificial Intelligence)` will create more jobs than what it destroys, or that people will
find more productive ways to spend their time[^a3].

[^a3]:
	> People are going to be more productive, they will have more time for leisure.
	>
	>--- {abbr}`Sundar Pichai, CEO Google (in New York Times, 22 May, 2026)`

Is this realistic, will people have more leisure time? By *leisure time* I don't mean *out of a job*, that is something
else. It is more likely that expectations will also grow. Increased productivity means greater profit for you and your
clients. If you happen to be in the consulting business your clients expect more for less and if your competitors are
willing to deliver, so must you. It is possible that you will work as hard as before but with less job satisfaction[^a4].

[^a4]: It is perhaps worth quoting the experience of Siddhant Khare at length,
	> AI genuinely makes individual tasks faster. That's not a lie. What used to take me 3 hours now takes 45 minutes.
	> Drafting a design doc, scaffolding a new service, writing test cases, 
	> researching an unfamiliar API. All faster.
	>
	> But my days got harder. Not easier. Harder.
	>
	> The reason is simple once you see it, but it took me months to figure out. When each task takes less time, you 
	> don't do fewer tasks. You do more tasks. Your capacity appears to expand, 
	> so the work expands to fill it. And then some. Your manager sees you shipping faster, so the expectations adjust. 
	> You see yourself shipping faster, so your own expectations adjust. The baseline moves.
	>
	> Before AI, I might spend a full day on one design problem. I'd sketch on paper, think in the shower, go for a 
	> walk, come back with clarity. The pace was slow but the cognitive load was manageable. One problem. One day. 
	> Deep focus.
	>
	> Now? I might touch six different problems in a day. Each one "only takes an hour with AI." But context-switching
	> between six problems is brutally expensive for the human brain. The AI doesn't get tired between problems. 
	> I do.
	> 
	>--- [Siddhant Khare on 8 February, 2026](https://siddhantkhare.com/writing/ai-fatigue-is-real)


### Mental Health
As far back as 2017 tech leaders expressed concern about the effect of technology on the human mind[^a5]. 

:::{pull-quote}
We hardened our financial institutions against hacking ... but it never occurred to 
us that the minds of our voters could be hacked; they turned our tools against us.

--- {abbr}`Roger McNamee, Managing Director of Elevation Partners (quoted in IEEE Spectrum, 14 November, 2017.)`
:::

[^a5]:
	> [Tech] companies began with a goal of connecting the world. But once they put a business model in place that 
	>depended on advertising, they had to put in techniques that depended on creating addiction.
	> Then when the smartphone came along, it created an opportunity to create a level of brain hacking that had never 
	> previously been seen.
	>
	> We hardened our financial institutions against hacking ... but it never occurred to us that the minds of our 
	> voters could be hacked; they turned our tools against us.
	>
	> --- {abbr}`Roger McNamee, Managing Director of the Venture Capital Firm Elevation Partners (in IEEE Spectrum, 14 November, 2017.)`

*Brain hacking* is of course nothing new. In *Influence: The Psychology of Persuasion*, for example, @cialdini2007 
describes how sales people hijack our natural and necessary psychological instincts to convince us to do stuff that we 
would not normally do. What is different now, is the scale and precision with which individuals are targeted. Sometimes
to devastating effect, as the widescale interference in the 2016 US election testifies, see for example, @snyder2018. 

Another [study](https://thenextweb.com/news/study-shows-dangerous-simple-manipulate-voters-dating-ai) points out the
ease with which humans are pursuaded by AI agents. Surely this is related to one of the psychological responses that
Cialdini describes in his book, namely, the trust in authority and how that natural psychological reaction can be 
hijacked to ill effect - trust in authority can be misplaced.

A study conducted at [Brown University](https://www.brown.edu/news/2025-10-21/ai-mental-health-ethics) indicates that
chatbots systematically violate mentall health ethics standards. 

It is worth emphasising the addiction to technology - tech companies still make most of their money from advertisements.
This in turn, means that the user has to be kept engaged and happy.
This is a theme that I'll return to later as it has consequences.



### Big Tech
Big tech has become truly gigantic with no end in sight. The present economic growth in the US is largely driven by 
big tech and specifically {abbr}`AI (Artificial Intelligence)` companies. With their companies behind them, the tech
leaders have become monstrously influential and ego-driven. Their decisions have planet-wide influence, and are not
always, perhaps rarely, in the best human interest. We are starting to see push-back, from resistance to develop new
data centers, to the booing of commencement speakers when they mention {abbr}`AI (Artificial Intelligence)`, to
Pope LeoXIV's encyclical, [Magnifica Humanitas](https://uscatholic.org/articles/202605/10-quotes-from-pope-leo-xivs-first-encyclical-magnifica-humanitas/).
Perhaps one can add the [EU AI Act of 2024](https://www.gdprsummary.com/gdpr-summary/?gad_source=1&gad_campaignid=11173866929&gbraid=0AAAAADR7W4VuBMDr-Pyt7gW5zasDy4PD6&gclid=CjwKCAjwhqfPBhBWEiwAZo196rOS8qfe2P05G1oSu5vhXlMuutNc2vvAxXFUvwgD-Wk3K-pNnIBDCxoCEpYQAvD_BwE)and the [General Data Protection Regulation](https://www.gdprsummary.com/gdpr-summary/), aiming to place limits on the use of {abbr}`AI (Artificial Intelligence)` and safeguarding the private data of individuals.

It is worth noting that the US is finding it extraordinally difficult to come up with similar legislation, and then
there are autocratic countries where the public enjoy no protection whatsoever.


### AI as a Mathematician
Quite recently OpenAI created quite a stir when on of their {abbr}`AI (Artificial Intelligence)` models solved an open
mathematics problem that researchers have been looking at for many years. For some, this is an indications the dream of 
{abbr}`AGI (Artificial General Intelligence)` is within reach, or has arrived.  What makes it even more impressive is
that a general {abbr}`LLM (Large Language Model)` was used meaning, the model was not specifically trained for solving
mathematical problems. It was a general-purpose model that just happened to be excellent at solving mathematical
problems as well. 

First a little background.

The Hungarian mathematician @erdos1946 proposed the so-called unit distance conjecture, the technical details need not
concern us here. Ever since mathematicians have tried to solve the conjecture without succcess [^a8]. That is, until
recently when the conjecture was disproofed by an internal model at OpenAI. Most of the following discussion is
based on @alon2026remarksdisproofunitdistance. 

[^a8]:
	>... there are several reasons that explain why AI tools can be better than humans in finding such
	> a construction. With or without a full agreement with these reasons, the fact is that the AI was
	> able to do here what lots of excellent human researchers tried and failed to do.
	>--- {abbr}`Noga Alon (in @alon2026remarksdisproofunitdistance.)`
	

To start off, I have no inside view on the OpenAI process involved in the disproof. To put it in proper context, it is
necessary to know, for example, how much and/or what type of human intervention was involved; was this the first problem
given to the model, or was this the one from many that the model was able to solve, etc.

Despite this, it is possible to demystify the proof a bit, just using the information, taken from @alon2026remarksdisproofunitdistance.

Quite a few things came together on this problem that falls naturally inside the strengths of
{abbr}`LLMs (Large Language Models)`. Here I can do no better than quote Thomas Bloom

1. Although many first class mathematicians worked on the problem, 

> most of the human efforts spent on this problem have been on
> trying to prove the upper bound, rather than spending serious time on trying to disprove it.
>
>On examining the construction, it becomes more clear how people had missed this before – it
> requires the confluence of several different unlikely events: that a good mathematician is
> (1) spending significant time in thinking about the unit distance conjecture in the first place;
> (2) seriously trying to disprove it, despite the oft-repeated belief of Erdős that it is true;
> (3) believes that there is mileage in generalising the original construction to other number fields,
> and so is willing to expend significant time in exploring such constructions; and
> (4) sufficiently familiar with the relevant parts of class field theory to recognise that the appro-
> priately phrased question about infinite towers of number fields with appropriate parameters
> can be solved using existing theory.
>
> The AI met all of these criteria, and its success here echoes previous achievements: it often produces
> the most surprising results by persevering down paths that a human may have dismissed as not
> worth their time to explore, combining superhuman levels of patience with familiarity with a vast
> array of technical machinery.
>--- {abbr}`Thomas Bloom(in @alon2026remarksdisproofunitdistance.)`

	AI agents are not indimidated by the reputation of great mathematicians, and have a sufficiently wide overview that lacks most individual researchers.

2. It is noticeable that a team of experts could verify the solution, simplefy it and generalise it somewhat,
	in a relatively short time. There seems to be consensus that the team of this paper, have the necessary background
	to have solved the problem themselves, if they had the incentive to get together to work in it.
3. Many mathematicians work on developing new theory, as opposed to solving problems. There is also a tendency
for silo-ing, as noted by Daniel Litt[^a9], 

> incentives towards specialization and silo-ing,though understandable,
> have cost us some high-quality science.
>
>In my own area—algebraic and arithmetic geometry—there are simply very few practitioners,
>and so arguably all problems are attention-bottlenecked. On the other hand, my sense is that it is
>unusual for well-known open problems to have answers accessible to clever argument, as opposed to
>new theory. Many of us work on programs, rather than problems.
>
--- {abbr}`Daniel Litt(in @alon2026remarksdisproofunitdistance)`


[^a9]: In @alon2026remarksdisproofunitdistance.

The remarks above do not mean to distract from the significance of the disproof of a major conjecture by an AI agent
and the reader is encouraged to consult @alon2026remarksdisproofunitdistance for more persepctive. 

I have, however, a few questions of my own and a bit of speculation.

In this case it was comparatively straightforward to verify the AI result. If, as is the expectation, AI will develop 
super human powers and come up with results where human mathematicians find it very hard or impossible to verify it,
what then? Will the results be accepted if, let's say, they were verified by another AI agent, keeping in mind that
both were trained on essentially the same data? Apart from the pleasure and the aesthetic satisfaction mathematicians 
derive from mathematics it does not appear to me a game that we play. Rather, is an endeavour by humans for humans.
In that sense it is very different from say, chess, where humans continue to compete agains one another, knowing full
well that they don't stand a chance against modern algorithms. Mathematics is not a competitive game against other
humans. To what extent are we willing to sacrifice human autonomy if, perhaps a big if, algorithms do become much
stronger mathematicians than the best humans?

## Review

In her book, *Machines that Think* Inga takes you, the reader, on a fascinating journey through the weird and wonderful world of AI.
She is not only pointing out the highlights but give you a peek behind the scenes. You will learn how the AI developers think about what they are doing,
what are the issues any developer must considered when developing and applying AI models. As a highly respected, active researcher she is a reliable guide she
is a reliable and enthusiastic guide through what is often a maze of conflicting opinions. She is enthusiastic about {abbr}`AI (Artificial Intelligence)` 
can offer, and has offered already, and is cautious when she must. 

One of the things many people will find surprising, even a little frightening, is that no one knows exactly how these complex models with their billions if not trillions of parameters, 'think'. We know in principle how the models are trained but it is much harder to know what design choices the developers inside the tech companies make. Few of the models are open sourced that will allow one to inspect the details. But the deeper reason is that machines and humans operate on very different principles[^a12]. The models are not designed to be human interpretable. If we do not understand how they 'think' it is much harder to anticipate how they 
will perform in challenging environments. What is the risk of unknow consequences.

[^a12]: Of course, statements like these are controversial. Some, like Geoffrey Hinton argues that the models are already conscious. (What are the implications if we accept this?) For me, Neil Lawrence (@lawrence2024) makes a strong case for differences between machine and human intelligence. 

Fortunately for the rest of us there are researchers like Inga who is researching this exact problem. This research field, known as {abbr}`XAI (Explainable Artificial Intelligence)` can succintly be described as {abbr}`AI (Artificial Intelligence)` forensics.  In this field, researchers dissect the internals of the large models to
figure out what it is that they actually learn. As mentioned above, this is crucial to understand where things can possibly go wrong, but is also crucial for learning from the models' learning. 

Although a book of about 250 pages, it is rich in information and deep in scope. You will learn about the technicalities behind the models - but don't despair, you will be carefully guided with brillaint explanations - but also what it means for us as individuals, for societies and even the broader impact on humanity. 

One of the constant themes throughout the book, is her emphasis of the role of *society* as a moderating influence on the future direction of AI. I'll discuss this fundamental issue in more detail, later. The emphasis on the importance of society and *civic duty* I referred to above, is, unfortunately, far from a universal value. Yet, the prevailing societal values determine to a large extent the path to the future, good or bad. AI therefore forces us to re-examine our basic values, as individuals, as societies

>Among the most fascinating aspects of articial intelligence as technology is how it forces us to contemplate difficult ethical dilemmas.
>
>--- {abbr}`Inge Strümke. (Machines that Think, p166.)`

### Ethics

Any discussion about the ethics of AI seems to start with the trolley problem. The variant used by Inga is the one of
an unstoppable trolley running along a railway track straight at 5 people fastened. onto the track. You, as a bystander,
happens to stand next to a control that will divert the trolley in time onto a different track. Sadly, on this track one
person is tied down and will sure be killed if you activate the control. The question is, what is the moral behaviour in
this situation, what will you do? For me, this problem is slightly awkward where there is no right answer. For me, I 
would like to have a third option and that is to try and stop the trolley, an option ruled out from the start. I find
support from @rawls1999 who rejects the unitarialism approach where one person is sacrificed for the greater good.
Instead, he poses, 
> Each person possesses an inviolability founded on justice that even the wellfare of society as a whole cannot override.
>
>--- {abbr}`John Rawls (in @rawla1999, p3.)`

Applying this the trolley problem to the AI controlling self-driving cars, the question becomes "Which life should be
prioritised"[^a11]? My personal response to this question is, it is the moral duty of the AI scientist to ensure that
this choice is not necessary by avoiding any collision at all cost, as I'm sure all of them do. As Inga points out,
machines, in a critical situation do not face the same limitations as humans. They do not become stressed and have a 
far faster response time. This means that they should avoid the choice, which one to kill, the elderly person in the
cross road, the baby, the passenger in the car?, until physics makes it impossible to avoid the collision. And if it is
not possible to avoid the collision what are the guidelines for the scientist implementing the decision? This is when
the scientist is faced with her own ethical and moral position. Ethics in AI is unavoidable.

[^a11]: Machines that Think, p167.

representative of the population where the model is implemented. We have also seen how hard it is to foresee when a
